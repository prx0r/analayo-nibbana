## SECTION 1: WHAT NIBBANA IS

### The theme verse (AN, the whole series' frame)
"Etam santam etam panitam: sabbasankharasamatho, sabbupadhipatinissaggo,
tanhakkhayo, virago, nirodho, nibbanam."
"This is peaceful, this is excellent, namely the stilling of all
preparations, the relinquishment of all assets, the destruction of
craving, detachment, cessation, extinction."
Alternative: "This is peaceful, this is sublime: the calming of all
constructions, the letting go of all supports, the extinguishing of
craving, dispassion, cessation, Nibbana."
**This is itself a meditation subject** (kammatthana). Reflection on the
peace of Nibbana (upasamanussati).

### The fire simile (the central metaphor)
- Fire burns with the help of firewood. "What is burning?" — the wood
  burns because of the fire, the fire burns because of the wood. This
  itself shows **idappaccayata** (this-to-that relatedness). The fire
  simile has very deep significance.
- Upadana means BOTH fuel and grasping. The fire grasps the wood; the
  wood grasps the fire. We call it "firewood." Upadana-paccaya-bhavo:
  dependent on grasping is existence. Two links in one image.
- When a fire goes out with exhaustion of fuel, it is **absurd to ask
  in which direction the fire has gone**. All one can say: "it comes to
  reckoned as gone out" (nibbuto tveva sankham gacchati). Just a
  reckoning, an idiom, a worldly usage — not to be taken too literally.

### The extinction evidence
- Ratanasutta: "Nibbanti dhiro yathayam padipo" — "Those wise ones get
  extinguished even like this lamp."
- Dhatuvibhanga-sutta: torch simile — "the mind's release was like the
  extinguishing of a torch."
- Asankhata-samyutta: **thirty-three terms** for the ultimate aim.
  Nibbana won out because of its fire allusion.
- Upasiva (Parayana): "Like the flame thrown by the force of the wind,
  reaches its end, it cannot be reckoned." / Bodhi: "goes out and cannot
  be designated, so the muni, liberated from the mental body, goes out
  and cannot be designated."
- "There is no measure of one who has gone out. There is no means by
  which they might speak of him. When all phenomena have been uprooted,
  all pathways of speech have also been uprooted."

### Tanhakkhayo IS nibbana (the synonym argument)
- In the suttas, tanhakkhayo (destruction of craving) is very often
  used AS a term for Nibbana.
- The commentator says: "Khayamatta na nibbanam" — destruction alone is
  not Nibbana. **Nanananda: this is a commentarial invention, not a
  sutta position.**
- Udana verse: "Whatever bliss from sense-desires there is in the world,
  whatever divine bliss there is — all these are not worth one-sixteenth
  of the bliss of the destruction of craving."
- Udapana verse: "What is the use of a well, if water is there all the
  time, having cut craving at the root, in search of what should one
  wander?" — destruction of craving is NOT a mere destruction.
- Craving is thirst; Nibbana is sometimes called pipasavinayo, the
  dispelling of thirst. "To think that the destruction of craving is
  not sufficient is like trying to give water to one who has already
  quenched his thirst."

### The arahants' own words
- Theragatha/Therigatha: "Sitibhuto'smi nibbuto" — "I am grown cool,
  extinguished as I am."
- The words sītibhūta and nibbuta "had a cooling effect even to the
  listener."
- "Extinction is something that occurs within an individual and it
  brings with it a unique bliss of appeasement."
- Ratanasutta: "Laddha mudha nibbutim bhunjamana" — "they experience
  the bliss of appeasement won free of charge." Normally appeasement is
  won at a cost; here it comes gratis.
- "One who has quenched his thirst for good is aware of that blissful
  experience. When he sees the world running here and there in search
  of water, he looks within and sees the well-spring of his bliss."

### The elevenfold fire
- "Existence is said to be an eleven-fold fire. So the entire existence
  is a raging fire. Lust, hate, delusion — all these are fires.
  Therefore Nibbana may be best rendered by the word 'extinction'."

### Warming vs cooling (Magandiya simile)
- The man with a skin disease sitting beside hot embers — trying to
  assuage pains by the heat of the fire. "It is an attempt to warm up,
  not to cool down."
- Lustful beings in the world are doing the same — warming up beside
  the fires of lust. "It can in no way be compared to the extinction
  and the cooling down of the Arahants."
- Buddhaghosa's heretic: if Nibbana is lust-extinction, animals have it
  too (they extinguish lust by enjoyment). **Refuted:** animals warm up;
  arahants cool down. Same word — "extinguishing" — two opposite
  processes.

### The permanent effect
- "It leaves a permanent effect on the Arahant, so much so that upon
  reflection he sees that his influxes are extinct, just as a man with
  his hands and feet cut off, knows upon reflection that his limbs are
  gone."

### Rādha chain (the path to nibbana)
- Venerable Radha's series of questions to the Buddha:
  1. Sammādassanam kimatthiyam? (For what purpose is right vision?)
     → "For purposes of disgust or dejection" (nibbidattham)
  2. Disgust is for dispassion (viragattham)
  3. Dispassion is for release (vimuttattham)
  4. Release is for Nibbana (nibbanattham)
  5. Nibbanam kimatthiyam? (For what purpose is Nibbana?)
     → "You have gone beyond the scope of your questions... For the
     holy life is merged in Nibbana, its consummation is Nibbana, its
     culmination is Nibbana." (nibbanogadham brahmacariyam vussati)
- "The holy life gets merged in Nibbana, just as rivers get merged in
  the sea. Where the holy life is lived out to the full, Nibbana is
  right there."
- Nanda (who took up the holy life for heavenly nymphs) attained
  Arahant-hood "almost in spite of himself." At last he begged the
  Buddha to relieve him of the onus of his promise. This shows: when
  one completes the training, one is already in Nibbana.

### The exam simile (the certificate)
- "In the case of an examination, after answering the question paper,
  one has to wait for the results to get a pass. Here it is different.
  As soon as you have answered the paper correctly, you have passed
  immediately and the certificate is already there."
- Anna (knowledge) = full certitude of the experience of Nibbana.
  Anaphala = the fruit of Arahantship. "One gets the certitude that
  birth is extinct and that the holy life is lived out to the full"
  (khina jati, vusitam brahmacariyam).

### The purpose question is incoherent
- "If there is any aim or purpose in attaining Nibbana, Nibbana would
  not be the ultimate aim. If Nibbana is the ultimate aim, there should
  no aim in attaining Nibbana. Though it may well sound a tautology,
  one has to say that Nibbana is the ultimate aim for the simple reason
  that there is no aim beyond it."
- Craving is forward-bent (bhavanetti, the leader in becoming). "It
  leads one on and on in existence, like the carrot before the donkey."
- Taṇhā ponobhavikā: craving brings up existence again and again.
  Infinite progression — this is for that, that is for the other.
- "When one makes the destruction of craving itself one's aim... when
  that aim is attained, there is nothing more to be done. So this brings
  us to the conclusion that tanhakkhayo is a full-fledged synonym of
  Nibbana."

### The Vedic dilemma (being and non-being)
- Katham asatah sat jayeta: "How could being come out of non-being?"
  The Vedic confusion between sat and asat.
- Nasadasi no sadasit tadanim: "There was neither non-being nor being
  at the start."
- The Buddha's solution: existence is a fire kept up by the fuel of
  grasping. When grasping ceases, existence ceases. **A perfect solution
  after a complete reappraisal.**

---

### Vacchagotta and the tetralemma (the accusation the fire answers)
- The accusation: "The wandering ascetic Vacchagotta, as well as many others, accused the Buddha of teaching a doctrine of annihilation: Sato sattassa ucchedaṃ vināsaṃ vibhavaṃ paññāpeti."
- "Their accusation was that the Buddha proclaims the annihilation, destruction and non-existence of a being that is existent. And the Buddha answered them fairly and squarely with the fire simile."
- The Buddha's answer: "Now if a fire is burning in front of you dependent on grass and twigs as fuel, you would know that it is burning dependently and not independently, that there is no fire in the abstract. And when the fire goes out, with the exhaustion of that fuel, you would know that it has gone out because the conditions for its existence are no more."
- "The fire simile holds the answer to many questions relating to the ultimate goal."
- The tetralemma: "In fact the fire simile holds the answer to the tetralemma included among the ten unexplained points very often found mentioned in the suttas. It concerns the state of the Tathāgata after death, whether he exists, does not exist, both or neither. The presumption of the questioner is that one or the other of these four must be and could be answered in the affirmative."
- "The Buddha solves or dissolves this presumptuous tetralemma by bringing in the fire simile. He points out that when a fire goes out with the exhaustion of the fuel, it is absurd to ask in which direction the fire has gone."
- "It is just a reckoning, an idiom, a worldly usage, which is not to be taken too literally. So this illustration through the fire simile drives home to the worldling the absurdity of his presumptuous tetralemma of the Tathāgata."
- "Here the reckoning is to be understood in terms of the four propositions of the tetralemma. Such reckonings are based on a total misconception of the phenomenon of fire."

### Upanishad cross-references (fire without fuel)
- Svetasvatara Upanisad 1.13 (Radhakrishnan): "As the form of fire when latent in its source is not seen and yet its seed is not destroyed, but may be seized again and again in its source by means of the drill"
- Maitri Upanisad 6.34 (Radhakrishnan): Even as fire without fuel becomes extinct in its own place, even so thought, by the cessation of activity, becomes extinct in its own source.
- Both are cross-references, not suttas: the fire-out-in-its-own-place
  intuition predates the Buddha, and Nanananda cites them as a sidelight to
  the depth of the argument (source sidebars).

## SECTION 2: HOW THE MECHANISM WORKS

### Idappaccayata (this-to-that conditionality)
- "The wood burns because of the fire and the fire burns because of
  the wood. So it seems we already have here a case of relatedness of
  this to that, idappaccayata."
- Upadana = fuel AND grasping. "The fire catches hold of the wood, and
  the wood catches hold of the fire. And so we call it firewood."
- Upadana-paccaya-bhavo: "dependent on grasping is existence. So long
  as there is grasping, there is an existence."

### The eternalists refuted
- "The eternalists, overcome by the craving for existence, thought that
  there is some permanent essence in existence as a reality."
- The Buddha: "what is true for the fire is true for existence as well.
  Existence is dependent on grasping. When grasping ceases, existence
  ceases as well."

## SECTION 3: WHAT KEEPS THE MECHANISM RUNNING

### Nama is name (not bending)
- In the suttas, nama used by itself means 'name'.
- The commentary (Paramatthajotika) explains it as 'bending' — immaterial
  states bend towards their objects. This is the standard Abhidhamma
  definition.
- "It may be that they thought it too simple an interpretation to
  explain nama with reference to 'name', particularly because it is a
  term that has to do with deep insight."
- "However as far as the teachings in the suttas are concerned, nama
  still has a great depth even when it is understood in the sense of
  'name'."

### The Sopaka story
- The ten questions Buddha put to the samanera Sopaka (attained
  Arahantship at age seven). "It is like asking a child: 'Can you count
  up to ten?'" All ten were deep, the tenth being on Arahantship. The
  second: "What is the 'two'?" — "Name and form."

### Name has conquered everything
- "Naman sabba anvabhavi, nama bhiyyo na vijjati, namassa ekadhammassa,
  sabbeva vasamanvagu."
- "Name has weighed down everything, nothing is more extensive than
  name. Name is the one thing that has all under its control."
- **Chinese parallel SA 1020** (CBETA, T02, no. 99): "名者映世間，名者
  世無上，唯有一名法，能制御世間。"

### The yoke of death
- "Akkheyyasannino satta, akkheyyasmit patitthita, akkheyyam aparinnaya,
  yogam ayanti maccuno."
- "Beings who perceive what can be expressed become established on what
  can be expressed. Not fully understanding what can be expressed, they
  come under the yoke of Death."
- "This shows that the word nama has a deep significance even when it
  is taken in the sense of 'name'."

### MN 9 definition (Sariputta)
- "Vedana, sanna, cetana, phasso, manasikaro — idam vuccatavuso, namam."
  (Feeling, perception, intention, contact, attention — this is name.)
- "Cattari ca mahabhutani, catunnanca mahabhutanan upadayarupam — idam
  vuccatavuso, rupam." (Four great primaries + form dependent on them —
  this is form.)
- Parallel MA 29 has the four immaterial aggregates instead.
- Ekottarika-agama parallel similar to MN 9 (Anālayo 2011: 70 n.220).

### The toddler and the rubber ball
- A toddler, unable to speak, given a rubber ball for the first time.
  Told "it is a rubber ball" — might not understand. How does he know
  it? He smells it, feels it, tries to eat it, rolls it on the floor.
  "At last he understands that it is a plaything."
- "The child has recognised the rubber ball not by the name that the
  world has given it, but by those factors included under 'name' in
  nama-rupa, namely feeling, perception, intention, contact and
  attention."
- "The world gives a name to an object for purposes of easy communication.
  When it gets the sanction of others, it becomes a convention."
- Commentator's illustration of the power of name: if a tree has no
  name, it would at least be known as the 'nameless tree.' But for the
  child, even such a usage is not possible.

### The meditator returns to childlike knowing
- "If a meditator wants to understand this name-and-form world, he has
  to come back to the state of a child, at least from one point of view.
  Of course in this case the equanimity should be accompanied by
  knowledge and not by ignorance."
- "That is why a meditator makes use of mindfulness and full awareness,
  satisampajanna, in his attempt to understand name-and-form."
- "All these have a specific value to each individual and that is why
  the Dhamma has to be understood each one by himself — paccattam
  veditabbo."
- "This Dhamma has to be realized by oneself. One has to understand
  one's own world of name-and-form by oneself. No one else can do it
  for him. Nor can it be defined or denoted by technical terms."
- "It may even appear as a process of unlearning down to childlike
  simplicity."

### Name is formal, form is nominal
- "'Name' in 'name-and-form' is a formal name. It is an apparent name.
  'Form' in 'name-and-form' is a nominal form. It is a form only in
  name."

## SECTION 4: WHAT THE MECHANISM BUILDS

### Nama-rupa is the wound
- "Now it is in this world of name-and-form that suffering is found.
  According to the Buddha, suffering is not out there in the conventional
  world of worldly philosophers. It is to be found in this very
  name-and-form world."
- "The ultimate aim of a meditator is to cut off the craving in this
  name-and-form. As it is said: acchecchi tanham idha namarupe."
  ("Cut off craving right here in name-and-form.")

### The surgeon simile
- The Buddha is the incomparable surgeon (sallakatto anuttaro). Also
  "tanasallassa hantaram" — one who removes the dart of craving.
- "Nama-rupa, or name-and-form, is like the wound in which the arrow
  is embedded. When one is wounded by a poison-tipped arrow, the
  bandage has to be put, not on the archer or on his bow-string, but on
  the wound itself. First of all the wound has to be well located and
  cleaned up."
- "Similarly, the comprehension of name-and-form is the preliminary step
  in the treatment of the wound caused by the poison-tipped arrow of
  craving."

### Rupa exists in relation to nama
- "Rupa exists in relation to 'name' and that is to say that form is
  known with the help of 'name'."
- "Many take nama-rupa to mean 'mind and matter.' Like materialists,
  they think there is a contrast between mind and matter. But according
  to the Dhamma there is no such rigid distinction."
- The four great primaries are the most primary notion of 'form'.
  "They are evasive like ghosts." But out of their interplay we get
  the perception of form (rupasanna).
- "What is called rupa in this context is rupasanna. It is with
  reference to the behaviour of the four great elements that the world
  builds up its concept of form."
- How the primaries are known: earth through hardness/softness, water
  through cohesiveness/dissolution, fire through hotness/coolness, wind
  through motion/inflation.

### The Jaṭā tangle (the disentangling)
- The deity's riddle: "Anto jata bahi jata, jataya jatita paja, tam tam
  Gotama pucchami, ko imam vijataye jatam."
- "There is a tangle within, and a tangle without, the world is entangled
  with a tangle. About that, oh Gotama, I ask you, who can disentangle
  this tangle?"
- Verse 1 (Visuddhimagga opening): a wise monk established in virtue
  disentangles it.
- Verse 2: arahants with lust, hate, ignorance faded — the tangle is
  disentangled in them.
- Verse 3 (relevant): "Yattha namanca rupanca asesam uparujjhati,
  patigham rupasanna ca, etthesa chijjate jata."
- "Where name and form, as well as resistance and the perception of
  form, are completely cut off, it is there that the tangle gets
  snapped."
- "The reference here is to Nibbana. It is there that the tangle is
  disentangled."

### Patigha = resistance (not repugnance)
- "Here patigha does not mean 'repugnance', but 'resistance'. It is the
  resistance which comes as a reaction to inert matter."
- "When one knocks against something in passing, one turns back to
  recognize it. Sense reaction is something like that."
- "The blind worldling recognizes an object by the very resistance he
  experiences in knocking against that object."
- "Patigha and rupasanna form a pair. Patigha is that experience of
  resistance which comes by the knocking against an object, and
  rupasanna, as perception of form, is the resulting recognition."

### Names perpetuate ignorance
- "Knowledge and understanding are very often associated with words and
  concepts, so much so that if one knows the name of a thing, one is
  supposed to know it. Because of this misconception the world is in a
  tangle."
- "Names and concepts, particularly the nouns, perpetuate the ignorance
  in the world. Therefore insight is the only path of release."
- "A meditator practically comes down to the level of a child in order
  to understand name and form. He may even have to pretend to be a
  patient in slowing down his movements for the sake of developing
  mindfulness and full awareness."

### The commentaries fail on deep suttas
- "Very often the commentaries are unable to say something conclusive
  regarding the meaning of deep suttas. So they simply give some
  possible interpretations and the reader finds himself at a loss to
  choose the correct one."
- "Sometimes the commentaries go at a tangent and miss the correct
  interpretation."
- Four historical reasons:
  1. Ani-sutta prophecy: monks will lose interest in deep suttas on
     emptiness.
  2. Precept vs realization debate: precept won. "For the continuity of
     the Sasana, precept itself is enough, not so much the realization."
  3. "The basket of the Buddha word came to be passed on from hand to
     hand in the dark, so much so that there was the risk of some
     valuable things slipping out."
  4. "There had been a tendency in the commentarial period to elaborate
     even on some lucid words in the suttas, simply as a commentarial
     requirement... By too much overdrawing in the commentaries, the
     deeper meanings of the Dhamma got obscured."
- **The depth is seen through lucidity:** "the depth of the Dhamma has
  to be seen through lucidity, just as much as one sees the bottom of a
  tank only when the water is lucid."

### The nibbana etymology refuted
- Common: "Vanasankhataya tanhaya nikkhantatta" — Nibbana is so called
  because it is an exit from craving which is a form of weaving.
- "To take the element vana in the word to mean a form of weaving is as
  good as taking nama in nama-rupa as some kind of bending."
- "But nowhere in the suttas do we get this sort of etymology."
- "On the other hand it is obvious that the suttas use the word Nibbana
  in the sense of 'extinguishing' or 'extinction'."

### Buddhaghosa's heretic refuted
- Visuddhimagga argument: if Nibbana is extinguishing of lust, animals
  also extinguish their fires of lust through enjoyment.
- "This argument ignores the deeper sense of the word extinction, as it
  is found in the Dhamma."
- "That man [with skin disease] is simply trying to assuage his pains
  by the heat of the fire. It is an attempt to warm up, not to cool
  down."
- "Similarly what the lustful beings in the world are doing in the face
  of the fires of lust is a warming up."

### The commentarial contradiction
- On one hand: Nibbana as exit from craving (weaving etymology).
- On the other: "it is on seeing Nibbana that craving is destroyed."
- "To project Nibbana into a distance and to hope that craving will be
  destroyed only on seeing it, is something like trying to build a
  staircase to a palace one cannot yet see."

### Dhammacakka definition of the third truth
- "Tassayeva tanhaya asesaviraganirodho cago patinissaggo mutti analayo."
- "The third noble truth is the complete fading away, cessation, giving
  up, relinquishment of that very craving. That it is the release from
  and non-attachment to that very craving."
- "In other words it is the destruction of this very mass of suffering
  which is just before us."

### Why Nanananda gave these sermons
- Three reasons: (1) the Great Preceptor invited him; (2) hope of benefit
  to co-dwellers in the Dhamma; (3) "I myself felt rather concerned about
  the inadequacy of the existing interpretations."
- The theme verse was chosen "to help create the correct atmosphere of
  meditative attention" rather than dealing with deep suttas "in academic
  isolation."

### The deep suttas the commentators let slide (Anisutta, pakińńakadesana)
- "There is a popular belief that the commentaries are finally traceable to a miscellany of the Buddha word scattered here and there, as pakiṇṇakadesanā. But the true state of affairs seems to be rather different."
- The Anisutta prophecy (Nidavagga, Samyutta Nikaya): "Ye te suttantā tathāgatabhāsitā gambhīrā gambhīratthā lokuttarā suññatappaṭisaṃyuttā, tesu bhaññamānesu na sussūssisanti na sotaṃ odahissanti na aññā cittaṃ upaṭṭhāpessanti na te dhamme uggahetabbaṃ pariyāpuṇitabbaṃ maññissanti."
- "It is said that in times to come, monks will lose interest in those deep suttas which deal with matters transcendental, that they would not listen to those suttas that have to do with the idea of emptiness, suññatā."
- "According to Manorathapūraṇī, the Aṅguttara commentary, already at an early stage in the Sāsana history of Sri Lanka, there had been a debate between those who upheld the precept and those who stood for realization."
- The conclusion: "For the continuity of the Sasana, precept itself is enough,
  not so much the realization."

### The elements as the world knows them (the primaries' behaviour)
- "The earth element is recognized through the qualities of hardness and
  softness, the water element through the qualities of cohesiveness and
  dissolution, the fire element through hotness and coolness, and the wind
  element through motion and inflation."
- "It is with reference to the behaviour of the four great elements that the
  world builds up its concept of form. Its perception, recognition and
  designation of form is in terms of that behaviour."
- Dual membership: this belongs to rupa (section 4) and to the practice of
  knowing the elements (see sermons 28 ff.).

### The second Jata verse (in whom the tangle is disentangled)
- "In whom lust, hate And ignorance have faded away, Those influx-free Arahants,
  It is in them that the tangle is disentangled." (Sn, Jatasutta - second of
  the three verses; the third, on name-and-form being cut off, is the one
  relevant to the topic and is quoted above.)
- Note: the first verse (sila, citta, panna) is the opening verse of the
  Visuddhimagga.

## SECTION 5: HOW TO SEE THROUGH IT

### The six qualities of the Dhamma (the seeing-frame of the whole series)
- "We are told that the Dhamma is svākkhāta, that it is wellproclaimed, sandiṭṭhika, can be seen here and now, akālika, timeless, ehipassika, inviting one to come and see, opanayika, leading one onwards, paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi, that it can be understood by the wise each one by himself."
- "This set of sermons would have fulfilled its purpose if it drives home the
  true significance of these six qualities of the Dhamma."
- The theme verse was selected as heading and theme "hoping that it would help
  create the correct atmosphere of meditative attention."
- The frame is therefore here-and-now seeing (sanditthika) plus each-one-for-
  himself understanding (paccattam vedittabbo) - not academic study. Contrast
  the invitation to rather than dealing with those deep controversial suttas in academic isolation..
- AN 10.60 (AN V 111, Bodhi 2012: 1413), the perception of non-delight:
  "What is the perception of non-delight in the world? Here a bhikkhu refrains
  from any engagement and clinging, mental standpoints, adherences, and
  underlying tendencies in regard to the world, abandoning them without
  clinging to them."

### The blind worldling and the Dhamma-eye
- "The Buddha has said that the worldling is blind until at least the Dhamma-eye
  arises in him. So the blind worldling recognizes an object by the very
  resistance he experiences in knocking against that object."
- "Out of such perceptions common to the blind worldlings, arises the
  conventional reality, the basis of which is the world."
- Dual membership: "Therefore insight is the only path of release" is said in
  section 4 (Names perpetuate ignorance); this block is where the seeing itself
  is thematized.

## SECTION 6: WHAT REMAINS

### Already in Nibbana; what more is needed?
- "According to the Buddha the cessation of existence is Nibbana and that means
  Nibbana is the realization of the cessation of existence."
- "When once the fires are extinguished, what more is needed?"
- "This shows that when one completes the training in the Holy Life, one is already in Nibbāna. Only when the training is incomplete, can one go to heaven." (dual membership: the completion claim also sits in section
  1, the Radha chain.)
- Thin by design: this sermon defines what remains in passing. The rest sits in
  section 1 (the arahants' own words: "I am grown cool, extinguished as I am";

## REFUTATIONS

### Annihilationism - Vacchagotta's accusation (section 1)
- Accused of teaching the annihilation, destruction and non-existence of a
  being that is existent (Sato sattassa ucchedam vinasam vibhavam panapeti).
  Answered with the fire simile: not preserved, not destroyed - gone out, and
  the question of which direction it had gone absurd. Full quotes filed in
  section 1 (Vacchagotta and the tetralemma).

### The commentarial agamma periphrasis (section 1)
- "With an undue grammatical emphasis they would say that it is on coming to
  that Nibbana that lust and other defilements are abandoned: Nibbanam agamma
  ragadayo khinati ekameva nibbanam ragakkhayo dosakkhayo mohakkhayo ti
  vuccati."
- "If they are synonyms, there is no need to make any discrimination with
  regard to some of them, by insisting on a periphrastic usage like agamma."
- Related: Sn 231, sahavassa dassanasampadaya, "it is on seeing Nibbana that
  craving is destroyed" - the contradiction with the weaving etymology is filed
  in section 1 (The commentarial contradiction).

### Khayamattam na nibbanam (section 1)
- "But the commentator says that destruction alone is not Nibbāna: Khayamattaṃ na nibbānaṃ."
- The excavation's verdict on this line: a commentarial invention, not a sutta
  position - the suttas use tanhakkhayo AS nibbana (section 1, Tanhakkhayo IS
  nibbana).

### Buddhaghosa's heretic and the Nibbanasutta (section 1)
- "First of all he gets the heretic to put forward the idea that the destruction
  of lust, hate and delusion is Nibbana. Actually the heretic is simply quoting
  the Buddha word, for in the Nibbanasutta of the Asankhatasamyutta the
  destruction of lust, hate and delusion is called Nibbana: Ragakkhayo,
  dosakkhayo, mohakkhayo - idam vuccati nibbanam."
- "The words ragakkhaya, dosakkhaya and mohakkhaya together form a synonym of
  Nibbana, but the commentator interprets it as three synonyms."

### The 'bending' reading of nama (section 3)
- The Paramatthajotika definition: "all immaterial states are called nama, in
  the sense that they bend towards their respective objects" and also because
  the mind has the nature of inclination (Arammanabhimukham namanato, cittassa
  ca natihetuto sabbampi arupam 'naman'ti vuccati).
- Sutta position: in the suttas also, nāma, when used by itself, means 'name'. (section 3, Nama is name).

### The 'weaving' etymology of Nibbana (section 1)
- "Vanasankhataya tanhaya nikkhantatta" - exit from craving-as-weaving.
- But nowhere in the suttas do we get this sort of etymology and interpretation. Filed in section 1 (The nibbana etymology refuted).

### Precept over realization (section 4)
- The Manorathapurani debate's conclusion - "for the continuity of the Sasana,
  precept itself is enough, not so much the realization" - is one of the four
  historical reasons for the commentaries' failure (section 4, The commentaries
  fail on deep suttas / The deep suttas the commentators let slide).
