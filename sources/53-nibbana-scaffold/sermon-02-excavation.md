## SECTION 1: WHAT NIBBANA IS

### Nibbana as island (Kappa dialogue)
Kappa asks: "Unto them that stand midstream, when the frightful floods
flow forth, to them in decay-and-death forlorn, an island, sire, may you
proclaim. An island which none else excels."
Buddha: "Owning naught, grasping naught: the isle is this, none else
besides. Nibbana, that is how I call that isle, wherein is decay decayed
and death is dead."
Akiñcanaṃ = owning nothing. Anādānaṃ = grasping nothing. "This is the
island, nothing else." Nibbāna = extinction of decay-and-death.

### Nibbana as the destination of the holy life
Anuttara brahmacariyapariyosāna: Nibbana is the supreme consummation of
the holy life. The standard phrase announcing a new Arahant: "In this
very life he realized by his own higher knowledge and attained to that
supreme consummation of the holy life for the purpose of which clansmen
of good family rightly go forth from home to homelessness."

### The straight path (the path IS nibbana)
"This path is called the 'straight' and the direction it goes is called
the 'fearless'." (Ujuko nāma so maggo, abhayā nāma sā disā.)

### Itivuttaka verse on immediacy
"To the learner, learning in pursuit of the straight path, first comes
the knowledge of destruction, and then immediately the certitude."
Anantarā: the attainment is immediate. Ānantarikā samādhi = the
concentration proper to the fruit of Arahantship.

### The streamwinner is not a distant glimpse
General belief: the sotāpanna's vision of Nibbana is like a glimpse of a
distant lamp on a road with many bends; he has negotiated the first bend.
But: "the norm of immediacy is applicable even to the knowledge of the
first path." One who attains stream-winning may be a beggar, an illiterate
person, or a seven-year-old child.

The epithets of the streamwinner (all testifying to arrival, not glimpse):
- Diṭṭhadhammo: he has seen the Dhamma, the truth of Nibbana.
- Pattadhammo: he has reached the Dhamma, arrived at Nibbana.
- Viditadhammo: he has understood the Dhamma.
- Pariyogāḷhadhammo: he has plunged into the Dhamma, dived into Nibbana.
- Tiṇṇavicikiccho: he has crossed over doubts.
- Vigatakathaṃkatho: his waverings are gone.
- Vesārajjappatto: he has attained to proficiency.
- Aparappaccayo satthusāsane: in regard to the teacher's dispensation
  he is not dependent on others. "He could attain to Nibbana even without
  another's help, though with the teacher's help he would attain it sooner."

### The three fetters abandoned at stream-entry
Sakkāyadiṭṭhi (self-hood view), vicikicchā (sceptical doubt),
sīlabbataparāmāsa (attachment to holy vows and ascetic practices).
Sīlabbata = "behavior and observances" (Bodhi). Dhp 271: "Not merely by
virtuous conduct and vows has a bhikkhu attained confidence, as long as
he has not attained the destruction of the āsavas."

### Nila's iron bar (the streamwinner's first blow)
King Gajabahu's soldier Nila parted the seawater with a huge iron bar.
"When the supramundane path arises in the mind the power of thought is
as mighty as the blow of Nila with his iron bar. Even with the first
blow the sea-water parted, so that one could see the bottom. Similarly
the sweeping influxes are parted for a moment when the transcendental
path arises in a mind, enabling one to see the very bottom — Nibbana."

### The Dhammapada stream-cutting verse
"Strive forth and cut off the stream, discard, oh Brahmin, sense-desires,
having known the destruction of preparations, oh Brahmin, become a knower
of the un-made."
"Just as one leaps forward and cuts off a stream of water, so it cuts
off, even for a moment, the preparations connected with craving. Thereby
one realizes the destruction of preparations."

### The asankhata is not "out there"
"What is called akata or asankhata, the un-made or the un-prepared, is
not something out there in a distance, as an object of thought. It is not
a sign to be grasped by one who wants to attain Nibbana. Language
encourages us to think in terms of signs. Very often we find it difficult
to get rid of this habit. The worldlings with their defilements have to
communicate with each other and the structure of the language has to
answer their needs. So the subject-object relationship has become a very
significant feature in a language."

### The Kakacudha dialogue (nothing to get, nothing to lose)
Deity: "Do you rejoice, oh recluse?" Buddha: "On getting what, friend?"
Deity: "Then do you grieve?" Buddha: "On losing what, friend?"
Deity: "You neither rejoice nor grieve!" Buddha: "That is so, friend."
"It seems, then, that though we say we 'attain' Nibbana there is nothing
to gain and nothing to lose. If anything — what is lost is an ignorance
that there is something, and a craving that there is not enough — and
that is all one loses."

### Other synonyms for nibbana
Akata (un-made), asankhata (un-prepared), sabbasankhara-samatha (stilling
of all preparations), dīpa (island). "Nibbana is not some mysterious state
quite apart from them. It is not something to be projected into a distance."

## SECTION 2: HOW THE MECHANISM WORKS

### The idappaccayata formula (the basic principle)
"This being, this comes to be. With the arising of this, this arises.
This not being, this does not come to be. With the cessation of this,
this ceases."
"It resembles an algebraical formula." The twelve-linked formula is the
illustration; the fourfold statement is the basic principle.

### Ariyapariyesana: the Buddha's two hard-to-see points
Soon after enlightenment, the Buddha reflected on the profundity of the
Dhamma and was disinclined to preach. He saw two points hard to see:
1. Paṭicca samuppāda: "Hard to see is this point, namely dependent
   arising which is a relatedness of this to that."
2. Nibbana: "And this point, too, is hard to see, namely the stilling
   of all preparations, the relinquishment of all assets, the destruction
   of craving, detachment, cessation, extinction."
Analayo parallel: MA 204 does not report Brahma's intervention (Anālayo
2011: 178ff).

### Idappaccayata as the term for dependent arising
"If there is any term we can use to define paṭicca samuppāda, a term
that comes closer to it in meaning, it is idappaccayatā."

### The Nidana-vagga distinction: dependent arising vs things arisen
Buddha: "Monks, I will teach you dependent arising and things that are
dependently arisen." He takes the last two links (birth → decay-and-death)
and draws attention to the basic principle:
"Whether there be an arising of the Tathagatas or whether there be no
such arising, that elementary nature, that orderliness of the Dhamma,
that norm of the Dhamma, the relatedness of this to that does stand as
it is."
Analayo parallel: SĀ 296. "Whether a Buddha emerges in the world or
whether he has not emerged in the world, this Dharma remains invariable."

### The commentary ignores the two-link sufficiency
The Buddha follows the two-link example with "this is the paṭicca
samuppāda" — showing the underlying principle could be understood with
just two links. But the commentary's definition takes the dependence
in a collective sense (plural "imesam").

### Every link is dependently arisen — including ignorance
The Buddha applies the qualifications (anicca, sankhata, paṭicca
samuppanna, khayadhamma, vayadhamma, viragadhamma, nirodhadhamma) to
each of the twelve links, "even ignorance." Decay-and-death themselves
are impermanent, prepared, dependently arisen, of a nature to get
destroyed, to pass away, fade away and cease.

### Birth is born? Death passes away?
"Birth itself is an arising. But here we can't help saying that birth
'arises'. It is like saying that birth is born. How can birth get born?
Similarly death is a passing away. But here we have to say that death
itself 'passes away'. How can death pass away?"

### Viraga in context = "fading away"
"In such contexts as avijjāvirāgā and pītiyā ca virāgā one has to render
it by words like 'fading away'. So that avijjāvirāga could be rendered
as: 'by the fading away of ignorance', and pītiyā virāgā would mean 'by
the fading away of joy'."

## SECTION 3: WHAT KEEPS THE MECHANISM RUNNING

### The Mahanidana sutta: name-and-form + consciousness as the rally point
"In so far only, Ananda, can one be born, or grow old, or die, or pass
away, or reappear, in so far only is there any pathway for verbal
expression, in so far only is there any pathway for terminology, in so
far only is there any pathway for designation, in so far only is the
range of wisdom, in so far only is the round kept going for there to be
a designation as the this-ness, that is to say: name-and-form together
with consciousness."
"Name-and-form together with consciousness is the rallying point for all
concepts of birth, decay, death and rebirth. All pathways for verbal
expression, terminology and designation converge on name-and-form
together with consciousness."

### The whirlpool: samsara literally means whirling round
"Vaṭṭa and āvaṭṭa are words used for a whirlpool... What is actually
meant by vaṭṭaṃ vattati is a whirling round, and samsara, even
literally, is that. Here we are told that there is a whirling round
between name-and-form and consciousness, and this is the samsaric
whirlpool to which all the aforesaid things are traceable."
Commentarial failure: "one Sinhala translation renders it as 'samsaric
rain'. What rain has to do with samsara is a matter for conjecture."

### How a whirlpool is formed (the full simile)
"Suppose a river is flowing downward. To flow downward is in the nature
of a river. But a certain current of water thinks: 'I can and must move
upstream.' And so it pushes on against the main stream. But at a certain
point its progress is checked by the main stream and is thrust aside,
only to come round and make a fresh attempt, again and again. All these
obstinate and unsuccessful attempts gradually lead to a whirling round.
As time goes on, the run-away current understands, as it were, that it
cannot move forward. But it does not give up. It finds an alternative
aim in moving towards the bottom. So it spirals downward, funnel-like,
digging deeper and deeper towards the bottom, until an abyss is formed.
Here then we have a whirlpool. While all this is going on, there is a
crying need to fill up the chasm, and the whirlpool develops the
necessary force of attraction to cater to it. It attracts and grasps
everything that comes within its reach and sends it whirling down, funnel
like, into the chasm."

### The whirlpool maps to dependent origination
"Ignorance on one side and craving on the other, as a result of the
abyss formed by the whirlpool. In order to satisfy this craving there is
that power of attraction: grasping. Where there is grasping, there is
existence, or bhava. The entire whirlpool now appears as a centre of
activity."

### The whirlpool in the body
"The basic principle underlying this whirlpool is to be found in our
bodies. What we call 'breathing' is a continuous process of emptying and
filling up. So even the so-called 'life-principle' is not much different
from the activity of a whirlpool. The functioning of the lungs and the
heart is based on the same principle and the blood circulation is in fact
a whirling round."

### The conflict is everywhere
"In our social life, rights and responsibilities go hand in hand... there
is a tangle within and a tangle without. In the field of economics...
production is governed by laws of supply and demand... the price
mechanism is on a precarious balance and that is why some wealthy
countries are forced to the ridiculous position of dumping their surplus
into the sea."

### The snake cycle
"Two snakes of the same size, trying to swallow up each other. Each of
them tries to swallow up the other from the tail upwards and when they
are half way through the meal, what do we find? A snake cycle. This
snake cycle goes round and round, trying to swallow up each other. But
will it ever be successful?"

### Dukkha etymology
"There is no 'auto'-ness even in the automatic. All that is there, is a
bearing up with difficulty. And this in fact is the meaning of the word
dukkha. Duḥ stands for 'difficulty' and kha for 'bearing up'. Even with
difficulty one bears it up, and though one bears it up, it is difficult."

## SECTION 4: WHAT THE MECHANISM BUILDS

### Itthabhava and annathabhava (this-ness and otherwise-ness)
"Ittha means 'this', so itthattaṃ would mean 'this-ness'. The whirling
of a whirlpool qualifies itself for a designation as a 'this'."

### Dvayatanupassana verses
"They that go on again and again the round of birth and death, which is
a thisness and an otherwise-ness — that going of them, that faring of
them, is only a journey of ignorance. The man with craving as his second,
faring on for a long time in samsara, does not get away from the round
which is a this-ness and an otherwise-ness."

### Birth = birth of a "here"
"One's birth is at the same time the birth of a 'here' or 'this place'.
And that is what is meant by itthabhava." Itthabhava and annathabhava
both mean 'this-ness' and 'otherwise-ness'. They go together.

### The commentary misses the deeper significance
"It explains itthabhavam as imaṃ manussabhāvam, 'this state as a human
being', and aññathābhāvam as ito avasesa aññanikāyabhāvam, 'any state
of being other than this'. This explanation misses the deeper significance
of the word itthatta."
Pāṭikasutta evidence: a being reborn in an empty Brahma mansion thinks
"How nice it would be if other beings also come to this state" — here
itthatta refers to the Brahma world, not the human world.

### The three characteristics as this-ness and otherwise-ness
Aniccatā (impermanence) explained with vipariṇāmaññathābhāva.
"Vipariṇāma is quite suggestive of a process of evolution. Strictly
speaking, pariṇāma is evolution... The prefix vi stands for the anti-
climax. The evolution is over, now it is becoming other."
AN Section-of-the-Threes: "An arising is manifest, a passing away is
manifest and an otherwise-ness in the persisting is manifest." The
persistence is only apparent — that is why it is mentioned last.

### Birth brings death as a matter of course
"As soon as one is born the process of otherwiseness sets in. Wherever
there is birth, there is death." Marasati verse: "Always death has come,
even with the birth itself." "Just as in a conjoined pair, when one is
drawn the other follows."

### The birthday-present
"Because of the pair-wise relationship, the very first 'birthday-present'
that one gets as soon as one is born is — death. The inevitable death
that he is entitled to."

### The whirlpool as false self
"Apparently it has the power to control, to hold sway. Seen from a
distance, the whirlpool is a centre of activity with some controlling
power. Now, one of the basic meanings of the concept of self is the
ability to control, to hold sway. And a whirlpool too, as seen from a
distance, seems to have this ability. Just as it appears automatic, so
also it seems to have some power to control. But on deeper analysis it
reveals its not-self nature."
Dhammapada: "Attā hi attano natthi" — "even oneself is not one's own."

### Nandavagga verses (the world as anguished)
"This anguished world, fully given to contact, speaks of a disease as
self. In whatever terms it conceives of, even thereby it turns otherwise.
The world, attached to becoming, given fully to becoming, though becoming
otherwise, yet delights in becoming. What it delights in is a fear, what
it fears from is a suffering. But then this holy life is lived for the
abandoning of that very becoming."

### Manana (conceiving) is the key word
"Manana is a word of deeper significance. Manana is conceiving under the
influence of craving, conceit and views. Whatever becomes an object of
that conceiving, by that very conception it becomes otherwise."

### Mara pursues what you grasp
"Whatever one grasps in the world, by that itself Mara pursues a being."
"Before a 'thing' becomes 'otherwise', it has to become a 'thing'. And
it becomes a 'thing' only when attention is focussed on it under the
influence of craving, conceit and views and it is separated from the
whole world and grasped as a 'thing'."

### The gems-in-the-pocket simile
"When one is walking through a town with one's pockets full of gems,
one is afraid because of the valuables in one's pockets. Even so, the
existence that one delights in is a source of fear. What one fears is
change or otherwise-ness, and that is suffering."

## SECTION 5: HOW TO SEE THROUGH IT

### The arahant cuts off the whirlpool
Udana Cula-vagga: "He has cut off the whirlpool and reached
desirelessness. The stream dried up now no longer flows. The whirlpool
cut off whirls no more. This, even this, is suffering's end."
"What has the Arahant done? He has cut off the whirlpool. He has breached
it and has reached the desireless state. The stream of craving is dried
up and flows no more."

### The deity dialogue: where elements find no footing
Deity: "From where do currents turn back, where whirls no more the
whirlpool, where is it that name-and-form is held in check in a way
complete?"
Buddha: "Where earth and water, fire and wind no footing find, from there
it is that currents turn back. There the whirlpool whirls no more and
there it is that name-and-form is held in check in a way complete."
"The four elements, which are like ghosts, do not haunt him. They do not
get a 'footing' in that consciousness."

### The arupa trap (running from the devil, taking it with you)
"Supposing someone, walking in the darkness of the night, has a
hallucination of a devil and runs away to escape from it. He thinks he
is running away from the devil, but he is taking the devil with him. The
devil is in his mind, it is something imagined. Similarly, until the
Buddha came into the scene, the worldlings grasped arupa in order to get
away from rupa. But because of the dichotomy between rupa and arupa,
even when they swung as far as the highest formless realms, they were
still in bondage to sankharas."

## SECTION 6: WHAT REMAINS

### The cessation formula
"Whatever is of a nature to arise, all that is of a nature to cease."
(Yam kiñci samudayadhammam sabbaṃ tam nirodhadhammam.)

### Why cessation is possible
"Because it is something arisen due to causes and conditions. Because it
is a process based on two things, without a self to hold sway. That is
why we have mentioned at the very outset that everything is impermanent,
prepared and dependently arisen."

### For the arahant, no whirlpool to designate
"For them there is no whirling round for there to be a designation:
vattam tesam natthi pannapanaya. So in their case, there is no whirling
round to justify a designation. This, then, is something deeper than the
whirlpool itself."

## REFUTATIONS

### The sankhata/asantkhata inversion argument
"Some are in the habit of getting down to a discussion on Nibbana by
putting sankhata on one side and asankhata on the other side. They start
by saying that sankhata, or the 'prepared', is anicca, or impermanent.
If sankhata is anicca, they conclude that asankhata must be nicca, that
is the unprepared must be permanent. Following the same line of argument
they argue that since sankhata is dukkha, asankhata must be sukha. But
when they come to the third step, they get into difficulties. If sankhata
is anatta, or not-self, then surely asankhata must be atta, or self. At
this point they have to admit that their argument is too facile."
"All this confusion arises due to a lack of understanding of the law of
Dependent Arising."

### The distant-lamp view of stream-entry
"There is a general belief that in the case of a sotapanna the vision of
Nibbana is like a glimpse of a distant lamp on a road with many bends
and the sotapanna has just negotiated the first bend. But in accordance
with the Dhamma it may be said that the norm of immediacy is applicable
even to the knowledge of the first path."

### The commentary on itthatta and vatta
Commentarial failure on vatta: one Sinhala translation renders it as
'samsaric rain'. Commentarial failure on itthatta: reduced to 'this human
world' when it means a generic this-ness. Both miss the deeper meaning.

## PASS 2 FINDS

- The Ariyapariyesana parallel (MA 204) — Nanananda notes Brahma's
  intervention is NOT in the Chinese parallel. Important for Analayo's
  comparative method.
- The commentary failure on vatta — "samsaric rain" — is a specific
  Sinhala translation error, not a general complaint. Worth keeping as
  a concrete example of commentarial drift.
- The Patikasutta evidence for itthatta — the Brahma world reference
  proves the word is generic, not "human world." This is a specific
  exegetical move, not a general observation.
- The arupa trap simile (running from the devil, taking it with you) —
  this is one of the best similes in the sermon and connects to the
  broader critique of formless attainments as bondage.
- The snake cycle — two snakes swallowing each other from the tail — is
  a distinct simile from the whirlpool. Both illustrate the same point
  but through different images.

## PASS 3: HOLES AND CONNECTIONS

- **Hole:** The sermon establishes that nibbana is the island, but doesn't
  fully explain what "owning nothing, grasping nothing" means in practice.
  That's left for later sermons.
- **Hole:** The relationship between the whirlpool (samsara) and the
  twelve links is established but not fully worked through. The sermon
  says "ignorance on one side, craving on the other" but the detailed
  mapping is left implicit.
- **Connection to Sermon 1:** The idappaccayata formula is the same
  principle as the fire simile — "wood burns because of fire, fire
  because of wood." Sermon 1 uses the fire image; Sermon 2 uses the
  whirlpool. Same dependent origination, different metaphors.
- **Connection to Sermon 3:** The whirlpool simile is explicitly picked
  up in Sermon 3 ("the other day, with the help of a simile of a
  whirlpool, we attempted an explanation of the terms samsara on the one
  hand, and Nibbana on the other").
- **Connection to Sermons 3-4:** The itthabhava/annathabhava pair is
  explicitly continued: "we were trying to explain how the process of
  the samsaric journey of beings could be understood even with the couple
  of terms itthabhava and annathabhava."
- **Analayo parallel worth noting:** MA 29 (the namarupa definition from
  Sermon 1) is referenced in Sermon 2's context — the Chinese parallel
  has the four immaterial aggregates instead of the five factors.
- **Connection to the VBT work:** The "running from the devil" simile
  maps to the VBT practices about not grasping at formless states. The
  arupa trap is the same insight as the signless practice: formless is
  still a form.
