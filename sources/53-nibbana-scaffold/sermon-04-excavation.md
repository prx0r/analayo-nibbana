Jnanaprasthana / Mahavibhasa.

## SECTION 1: WHAT NIBBANA IS

### Nibbana = unattached, no wavering, no inclination
Channovadasutta: "To the one attached, there is wavering. To the
unattached one, there is no wavering. When there is no wavering, there
is calm. When there is calm, there is no inclination. When there is no
inclination, there is no coming and going. When there is no coming and
going, there is no death and birth. When there is no death and birth,
there is neither a 'here' nor a 'there' nor a 'between the two'. This
itself is the end of suffering."
Analayo parallel: SĀ 1266 has the same chain in Chinese.
The Udana version: "The Buddha was instructing, inciting and gladdening
the monks with a Dhamma talk connected with Nibbana."

### Mara defeated by letting go
"When Maracomes round to grab, if we can find some means of foiling his
attempt, or make it impossible for him to grab, then Mara will have to
accept defeat."
"In a tug-of-war, when someone is pulling at one end with all his might,
if the other suddenly lets go of its hold, one can well imagine the
extent of the former's discomfiture, let alone victory."
"Mara's forte lies in seizing and grabbing. He is always out to
challenge. Sometimes he takes delight in hiding himself to take one by
surprise, to drive terror and cause horripilation."

### The Buddha's solution: letting go
"There are in the world various means of preventing others from grabbing
something we possess. We can either hide our property in an inaccessible
place, or adopt security measures, or else we can come to terms and sign
a treaty with the enemy. But all these measures can sometimes fail.
However, there is one unfailing method, which in principle is bound to
succeed. A method that prevents all possibilities of grabbing. And that
is — letting go, giving up. When one lets go, there is nothing to grab."
"It is a case of giving up all assets, sabbupadhipatinissagga."

## SECTION 2: HOW THE MECHANISM WORKS

### The three-lives vs one-mind-moment framework
Header: Patisambhidamagga / Vibhanga / Jnanaprasthana / Mahavibhasa.
The sermon addresses whether dependent origination reads across three
lives or within one mind-moment. Both frames are valid.

### The itthabhava/annathabhava circuit
"This-ness and otherwise-ness imply a circuit. It is a circuit between
a 'here' and a 'there'. It is a turning round, an alternation or a
circuitous journey. It is like a rotation on the spot. It is an
ambivalence between a here and a there."

### Consciousness travel = change of point of view
"The passage of consciousness from here to a mother's womb is not a
movement from one place to another, as in the case of the body. In
reality, it is only a difference of point of view, and not a transmigration
of a soul."
"When consciousness leaves this body and comes to stay in a mother's
womb, when it is fully established there, 'that' place becomes a 'this'
place. From the point of view of that consciousness, the 'there' becomes
a 'here'. Consequently, from the new point of view, what was earlier a
'here', becomes a 'there'."
"That way, what actually is involved here, is a change of point of view.
So it does not mean completely leaving one place and going to another,
as is usually meant by the journey of an individual. The process, then,
is a sort of going round and round."

### Even consciousness is dependently arisen
"There are instances in which the view that this selfsame consciousness
fares on in samsara by itself, tadevidam vinnanam sandhavati samsarati,
ananjam, is refuted as a wrong view."

### The Dhammapada tree simile
"Just as a tree, so long as its root is unharmed and firm, though once
cut down, will none the less grow up again, even so, when craving's
latency is not yet rooted out, this suffering gets reborn again and again."

### Kamma as field, consciousness as seed, craving as moisture
"Kamma is the field, consciousness is the seed, craving is the moisture."
"Consciousness grows in the field of kamma with craving as the moisture."

### Sela Thera's riddle and answer
Mara asks: "By whom was this image wrought, who is the maker of this
image, where has this image arisen, and where does the image cease?"
Sela: "Neither self-wrought is this image, nor yet other-wrought is this
misery. By reason of a cause, it came to be, by breaking up the cause,
it ceases to be. Just as in the case of a certain seed, which when sown
on the field would feed on the taste of the earth and moisture, and by
these two would grow. Even so, all these aggregates, elements and bases
six, by reason of a cause have come to be, by breaking up the cause will
cease to be."
Analayo parallel: SĀ 1203 attributes it to Vira instead of Sela. The
Chinese version adds: "I am entirely separated from the darkness of
ignorance, having realized the quietude of extinction, I dwell in peace."

### The cessation of consciousness = cessation of the prepared consciousness
"Some seem to think that the cessation of consciousness occurs in an
arahant only at the moment of his parinibbana, at the end of his life
span. But this is not the case. Very often, the deeper meanings of
important suttas have been obliterated by the tendency to interpret the
references to consciousness in such contexts as the final occurrence of
consciousness in an arahant's life — carimaka vinnana."
"What is called the cessation of consciousness has a deeper sense here.
It means the cessation of the specifically prepared consciousness,
abhisaikhata vinnana. An arahant's experience of the cessation of
consciousness is at the same time the experience of the cessation of
name-and-form."

### Munisutta: the sage who does not water the seed
"Having surveyed the field and measured the seed, he waters it not for
moisture. That sage in full view of birth's end, lets go of logic and
comes not within reckoning."
"By virtue of his masterly knowledge of the fields and his estimate of
the seed of consciousness, he does not moisten it with craving. Thereby
he sees the end of birth and transcends logic and worldly convention."

## SECTION 3: WHAT KEEPS THE MECHANISM RUNNING

### Anusaya (latency) is the key
"Anusaya, or latency, is a word of special significance. What is
responsible for rebirth, or punabbhava, is craving, which very often
has the epithet ponobhavika attached to it. The latency to craving is
particularly instrumental in giving one yet another birth to fare on
in samsara."
"There is also a tendency to ignorance, which forms the basis of the
latency to craving. It is the tendency to get attached to worldly
concepts, without understanding them for what they are."

### Nissaya (leaning on) is the deeper term
"Nissaya means 'association'. As a matter of fact, here it does not
have the same sense as the word has in its common usage. It goes deeper,
to convey the idea of 'leaning on' something. Leaning on is also a form
of association."
"Worldlings have a tendency to tenaciously grasp the concepts in worldly
usage, to cling to them dogmatically and lean on them. They believe that
the words they use have a reality of their own, that they are categorically
true in their own right. Their attitude towards concepts is tinctured by
craving, conceit and views."

### Kaccayanagotta: right view and the two extremes
"This world, Kaccayana, for the most part, bases its views on two things:
on existence and non-existence. Now, to one who with right wisdom sees
the arising of the world as it is, the view of non-existence regarding
the world does not occur. And to one who with right wisdom sees the
cessation of the world as it really is, the view of existence regarding
the world does not occur."
"'Everything exists' is one extreme. 'Nothing exists' is the other
extreme. Not approaching either of those extremes, the Tathagata teaches
the Dhamma by the middle way" — and then the twelve-link formula.
Analayo parallel: CBETA T02 no. 99.

### The four stages of dogmatic involvement
"Upaya means 'approaching', upadana is 'grasping', abhinivesa is 'entering
into', and vinibandha is the consequent entanglement. The implication
is that the worldling is prone to dogmatic involvement in concepts
through the stages mentioned above in an ascending order."

### Aparappaccaya nana = knowledge not dependent on another
"He is not believing in it out of faith in someone, but has understood
it experientially. The noble disciple sees the arising and the cessation
of the world through his own six sense bases."

### The six sense bases = the world
"In the six the world arose, in the six it holds concourse, on the six
themselves depending, in the six it has its woes."
Commentarial failure: the commentators interpret "six" alternating
between internal and external bases. "Even the grammar does not allow
it, for if it is a case of associating 'with' the external sense bases,
the instrumental case would have been used instead of the locative
case." The reference is to the six sense bases of contact (phassayatana),
which include both internal and external.

### Samudayasutta: two are necessary for dependent arising
"Dependent on the eye and forms arises eye consciousness; the coming
together of the three is contact."
"This shows that two are necessary for a thing to be dependently arisen."

### The world in the noble one's discipline
"Ariyassa vinaye loko — the world in the noble one's discipline. The
world is always defined in terms of the six sense bases, as if the world
arises because of these six sense bases. This is a very deep idea. All
other teachings in this Dhamma will get obscured, if one fails to
understand this basic fact."

### Tathata, avitathata, anannathata (the eternal law)
"From ignorance as condition, preparations come to be. That suchness
therein, the invariability, the not-otherwiseness, the relatedness of
this to that, this, monks, is called dependent arising."
"Tathata asserts the validity of the law of dependent arising, as a norm
in accordance with nature. Avitathata, with its double negative,
reaffirms that validity to the degree of invariability. Anannathata, or
not-otherwiseness, makes it unchallengeable, as it were. It is a norm
beyond contradiction."

## SECTION 4: WHAT THE MECHANISM BUILDS

### The two bulls simile (the conjoined pair)
"Suppose two bulls, a black one and a white one, are bound together at
the neck and allowed to graze in the field as a pair... If the white
bull pulls towards the stream, while the black one is pulling towards the
field, there is a conflict. The conflict is not due to the bondage, at
least not necessarily due to the bondage. It is because the two are
pulling in two directions."
"Supposing the two bulls, somehow, accept the fact that they are in
bondage and behave amicably. When then the white bull pulls towards the
stream, the black one keeps him company with equanimity, though he is
not in need of a drink. And when the black bull is grazing, the white
bull follows him along with equanimity, though he is not inclined to eat."
"The conflict is resolved by accepting the pair-wise combination as a
conjoined pair."

### The tree-seller simile (standpoint determines the concept)
"A person who makes his living by selling the leaves of a particular
kind of tree. Suppose another man sells the flowers of the same tree...
And yet another sells the fruits, while a fourth sells the timber. If
we line them up and put to them the question, pointing to that tree:
'Is this tree mature enough?', we might sometimes get different answers.
Each would voice his own commercial point of view."
"The concept of decay or old age can change according to the standpoint
taken up. From beginning to end, it is a process of decay. But we create
an artificial boundary between youth and old age."

### Kosiya the miser (the rice-cakes string)
"The millionaire Kosiya, an extreme miser, once developed a strong desire
to eat rice-cakes. As he did not wish to share them with anyone else,
he climbed up to the topmost storey of his mansion with his wife... To
teach him a lesson, Venerable Mahamoggallana went through the air and
appeared at the window... Further attempts to make tinier rice-cakes
ended up in producing ever bigger and bigger ones. In the end, all the
cakes got joined to each other to form a string of cakes. The couple
then started pulling this string of cakes in either direction with all
their might, to separate just one from it. But without success. At last
they decided to let go and give up, and offered the entire string of
cakes to the venerable Thera."
"The Buddha's solution to the above problem is a similar let go-ism and
giving up."

### Birth and decay-and-death are a conjoined pair
"So long as one does not accept the fact that these two links, birth
and decay-and-death, are a conjoined pair, one would see between them a
conflict. Why? Because one grasps birth as one end, and tries to remove
the other end, which one does not like, namely decay-and-death. One is
trying to separate birth from decay-and-death. But this happens to be
a conjoined pair."
"Jarā, or decay... implies both growth and decay, as it sets in from
the moment of one's birth itself."

### The Satipatthana thematic paragraph
"In this way he abides contemplating the body as a body internally, or
he abides contemplating the body as a body externally, or he abides
contemplating the body as a body internally and externally. Or else he
abides contemplating the arising nature in the body, or he abides
contemplating the dissolving nature in the body, or he abides contemplating
the arising and dissolving nature in the body. Or else the mindfulness
that 'there is a body' is established in him only to the extent necessary
for just knowledge and further mindfulness. And he abides independent
and does not cling to anything in the world."
Analayo parallel: MĀ 98 and EĀ 12.1.
"It is this paragraph that is called satipatthana bhavana, or meditation
on the foundation of mindfulness. The preamble deals with the foundation
itself. The above paragraph deals with what pertains to insight. It is
the field of insight proper."

### Internal and external are neighbours
"The farthest limit of the internal is the nearest limit of the external.
The farthest limit of the external is the nearest limit of the internal."
"Whatever portion one demarcates as one's own, just adjoining it and at
its very gate is bahiddha. And from the point of view of bahiddha, its
farthest limit and at its periphery is ajjhata. This is a conjoined
pair. These two are interrelated. So the implication is that these two
are not opposed to each other."

### Sunrise and sunset (udaya and atthagama)
"Udaya means sunrise, and atthagama is sunset. The farthest limit of the
forenoon is the nearest limit of the afternoon. The farthest limit of
the afternoon is the nearest limit of the forenoon. And here again we
see a case of neighbourhood."

### The raft simile
"Nitharanatthaya no gahanatthaya — for crossing over and not for holding
on to."
"What we have here are so many scaffoldings for the up-building of
mindfulness and knowledge. Probably due to the lack of understanding of
this deep philosophy enshrined in the Satipatthanasutta, many sects of
Buddhism took up these concepts in a spirit of dogmatic adherence. That
dogmatic attitude of clinging on is like the attempt to cling on to the
scaffoldings and to live on in them."

## SECTION 5: HOW TO SEE THROUGH IT

### The Satipatthana four modes (the practice frame)
1. Internally (ajjhata)
2. Externally (bahiddha)
3. Both (ajjhattabahiddha)
4. Arising (samudaya)
5. Dissolving (vaya)
6. Both (samudayavaya)
7. "There is a body" — only for knowledge and mindfulness
8. Independent, not clinging to anything in the world (anissita)

"The farthest limit of the internal is the nearest limit of the external.
Attend to them both together, as ajjhattabahiddha, and that dogmatic
involvement with a view is abandoned. Here we have an element of
reconciliation, which prevents adherence to a view."

### The Kaccayanagotta practice
"Whoever does not approach, grasp, and take his stand upon that
proclivity towards approaching and grasping, that mental standpoint,
namely the idea: 'This is my soul', he knows that what arises is just
suffering and what ceases is just suffering."

## SECTION 6: WHAT REMAINS

### Beyond the extremes
"'Everything exists' is one extreme. 'Nothing exists' is the other
extreme. Not approaching either of those extremes, the Tathagata teaches
the Dhamma by the middle way."

### The end of suffering (Channovadasutta)
"When there is no death and birth, there is neither a 'here' nor a
'there' nor a 'between the two'. This itself is the end of suffering."

## REFUTATIONS

### The commentary on the six-sense-bases verse
The commentators alternate between internal and external bases. "Even
the grammar does not allow it." The locative chasu occurs in all three
lines. The reference is to the six sense bases of contact, including
both internal and external.

### The three-lives-only reading
The sermon doesn't reject it but shows that the one-mind-moment frame
is equally valid and more directly verifiable. The Patisambhidamagga
and Vibhanga both support the momentary reading.

### The carimaka vinnana interpretation
"The deeper meanings of important suttas have been obliterated by the
tendency to interpret the references to consciousness in such contexts
as the final occurrence of consciousness in an arahant's life." The
cessation of consciousness is the cessation of the specifically
prepared consciousness, not the final moment of an arahant's life.

### Dogmatic adherence to Satipatthana concepts
"Many sects of Buddhism took up these concepts in a spirit of dogmatic
adherence. That dogmatic attitude of clinging on is like the attempt to
cling on to the scaffoldings and to live on in them."

## PASS 2 FINDS

- The Sela Thera attribution — SĀ 1203 attributes the verse to Vira,
  not Sela. The Chinese version adds a liberation verse not in the Pali.
- The Channovadasutta parallel (SĀ 1266) — the Chinese version has the
  chain in slightly different order but same logic.
- The grammar argument on the six-sense-bases verse — Nanananda uses
  locative vs instrumental case to refute the commentary. This is a
  specific linguistic move.
- The Kosiya story — one of the best similes in the series. The rice-
  cakes string is unforgettable.
- The tree-seller simile — four people with four different answers to
  "is this tree mature?" Perfect for the standpoint problem.
- The Munisutta verse — "having surveyed the field and measured the seed"
  — connects directly to the kamma/seed/moisture formula.

## PASS 3: HOLES AND CONNECTIONS

- **Hole:** The sermon establishes that nissaya (leaning on) is the key
  term but doesn't fully explain HOW to stop leaning. That's the practice
  question, left for the satipatthana sermons.
- **Hole:** The relationship between anusaya (latency) and the practice
  of uprooting it is established (Dhammapada tree simile) but not worked
  through.
- **Connection to Sermon 3:** The Cetasutta three instances (intention,
  latency, neither) is the practice that leads to the cessation of the
  prepared consciousness. The Mahanidana womb catechism is continued.
- **Connection to Sermon 2:** The whirlpool simile is the same. The
  idappaccayata formula is the same. The tathata/avitathata/anannathata
  is the eternal law version of the same principle.
- **Connection to Sermon 5:** The Satipatthana thematic paragraph IS the
  practice frame. Sermon 5 develops it fully.
- **Connection to Sermons 7-8:** The anidassana vinnana is the "unestablished
  consciousness" of Channovadasutta. The arahant's consciousness has no
  wavering, no inclination.
- **Connection to VBT pack:** The "neither intention nor construction nor
  latency" practice maps to the signless concentration. The Kaccayanagotta
  right view practice maps to the recognition contemplation.
